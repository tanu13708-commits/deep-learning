# 02 — Activation Functions

## 9. Why do neural networks need activation functions?
Activation functions introduce **non-linearity**. Without nonlinear activations, stacking linear layers would still produce an overall linear transformation, limiting the network's ability to learn complex relationships.

## 10. Explain Sigmoid and its limitations.
**σ(x) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ˣ)**

Sigmoid outputs values between 0 and 1 and is useful for a binary probability output. Limitations include saturation for large positive/negative inputs, very small gradients in saturated regions, and non-zero-centered outputs.

## 11. Explain Tanh and its limitations.
**tanh(x) = (eˣ - e⁻ˣ)/(eˣ + e⁻ˣ)**

Tanh outputs values from -1 to 1 and is zero-centered. Like sigmoid, it can saturate and produce small gradients for large magnitudes.

## 12. Explain ReLU and why it became widely used.
**ReLU(x) = max(0, x)**. It is simple, computationally efficient, and does not saturate for positive inputs. It became a common hidden-layer activation because it often trains deep networks more effectively than sigmoid/tanh.

## 13. What are Leaky ReLU and other ReLU variants?
Leaky ReLU gives negative inputs a small slope: **f(x)=x if x>0, otherwise αx**. Other variants include PReLU, ELU, SELU and GELU. They modify ReLU behavior to improve gradient flow or optimization in particular settings.

## 14. How do you choose an activation function?
Common choices: ReLU or variants for many hidden layers; sigmoid for a single binary probability output; softmax for multi-class single-label output; linear/no activation for many regression outputs; GELU or related smooth activations are common in Transformers. The task and architecture determine the final choice.
